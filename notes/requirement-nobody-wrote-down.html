The Requirement Nobody Wrote Down

August 2026

Human-in-the-loop is a product requirement. Specified before launch. Not a safety feature bolted on after something breaks.

Three different healthcare AI companies could each point to a human in the loop, a review process, a compliance layer. None of them had someone who'd actually done discovery, the unglamorous work of asking who this touches and what breaks it, before they built. This is product 101.

The data work wasn't the failure. Finding that antibiotic timing tracked with sepsis outcomes, that length of stay clustered by diagnosis, that certain claims got overturned on appeal, that's legitimate. That's where a real product idea should start. The failure is what happened next: nothing. They found a pattern worth building on and skipped straight to building, without ever doing the rest. What does this pattern look like in a real case, not an aggregate. How does the current workflow handle it today, what do people already do, work around, or compensate for. And only then, where could a tool intervene in a way that helps instead of just adding another thing to react to. That sequence didn't happen, or didn't happen enough, in any of the three.

None of this is new to me. I built this exact constraint into a shipped, certified EHR product years before AI made it fashionable, keep the decision with the clinician, remove the work around it. The receipt is here.

Epic's sepsis model

Epic's original sepsis model validated its own accuracy against billing codes, not clinical criteria. Billing codes get finalized after discharge, sometimes days later, by coders reading a chart for reimbursement, not by anyone tracking when a patient's condition turned. When an outside team at Michigan checked the model against a CDC clinical surveillance definition of sepsis, the numbers collapsed. AUC of 0.63. Sensitivity of 33 percent. Nearly one in five hospitalized patients got an alert, and when it fired, the patient actually had sepsis about 12 percent of the time, roughly eight evaluations for every real case.

Part of the model's apparent accuracy came from antibiotic orders as an input feature. A clinician orders antibiotics when they already suspect infection. So the model was partly scoring what a doctor had already acted on, and Epic sold the output as independent prediction.

This ran in hundreds of hospitals with no FDA clearance. Not a lighter review track, no review at all. Clinical decision support tools like this typically need Class II clearance. Epic never went through that gate, and nobody outside the company could check the training data, the ground truth, or the leakage before it touched a patient. The correction came roughly five years later, from an outside academic team, not from any built-in monitoring loop.

nH Predict

UnitedHealth's naviHealth ran a post-acute care algorithm to project how long a patient should need rehab. The override existed on paper. A case manager could still authorize more days if a patient needed them. What removed the override wasn't the model, it was the incentive sitting on top of it: a 2023 target to keep stays within 1 percent of what the algorithm projected, with discipline or termination for missing it. One former case manager described the choice as job performance against conscience.

CMS didn't issue AI-specific guidance for Medicare Advantage coverage decisions until February 2024, after the class action was filed, and even that guidance addressed the algorithm's role in coverage denials, not the compensation structure that broke the override. Congress flagged the same gap months later. The regulator showed up, eventually, and still missed the mechanism that mattered.

Virtix Health

Virtix is one of six vendors in CMS's WISeR pilot, using AI to review Medicare prior authorization requests. In June, CMS ordered a corrective action plan after Virtix missed the pilot's 72-hour turnaround requirement, an operational failure, not a data or security one.

The detail worth sitting with is buried one paragraph into the coverage: Virtix is compensated based on a share of averted Medicare expenditures. Paid on money saved, not on getting the determination right, inside a program built, on paper, around clinical accuracy rather than cost savings. Same shape as nH Predict. Different layer. Nobody wrote "the entity making this call cannot be paid in a way that rewards one outcome over another" into the program design before launch, again.

The pattern

Three companies. Three different mechanisms. Wrong ground truth. A removed override. A misaligned payout. Whether this was a deliberate bet that speed beats scrutiny, or a genuine blind spot, I can't say, and I'm not sure it matters. What's constant across all three is the missing step: nobody sat down before launch and asked what breaks, for whom, under what pressure.

AI isn't replacing PMs. It's taking the story-writing off our plates, the part that was never the hard part anyway. That frees us up for the part that actually matters, discovery, the judgment calls, knowing what to ask and who to ask it of. Three companies just proved what happens when nobody does that work. Bring us in before you build, not after. We fix it either way. One version is cheaper.
